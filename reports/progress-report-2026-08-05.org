#+TITLE: Hypervariorum: Progress Report and Next Steps
#+DATE: 2026-08-05
#+AUTHOR: Clifford Wulfman
#+OPTIONS: toc:nil num:nil

The first phase of the Hypervariorum project — converting the NVS
variorum transcriptions into structured, machine-readable annotations
— is now complete. All eight plays in the corpus are processed through
a two-stage pipeline that extracts commentary blocks from the raw
transcriptions and consolidates them into well-formed XML. Each
annotation carries act, scene, and line metadata; the NVS inline
typography (smallcaps for cited scholars, italic for titles and foreign
terms) is represented as real XML elements, making the citation
apparatus navigable by XPath for the first time.

Here is a sample annotation:

The process entailed only one significant challenge: comments that
spanned several pages were inconsistently marked in the raw
transcriptions, requiring some sophisticated parsing.  After
investigation and correction, final annotation counts range from
approximately 750 (/Romeo and Juliet/) to approximately 1,900 (/Hamlet/),
with a combined total across the seven commentary volumes of roughly
8,900 annotations.

With trustworthy annotation boundaries established, work has turned to
citation resolution — linking the smallcaps-tagged scholar names in
Furness's critical commentary to the sources they denote. A pilot study
on the approximately 71 bare-colon citations in /King Lear/ resolved
roughly 90% through bibliographic means: direct matches in Furness's
own bibliography, identification of historically excluded editors
(Johnson, Steevens, Malone), and cross-reference with standard
variorum sources. The remaining cases — approximately 83 instances
across the volume — require full-text search to disambiguate, the
clearest example being the several nineteenth-century scholars who
published under the name "Davies."

More significant than the unresolved cases, however, is what the pilot
revealed about citation structure. Furness's citations are not
formally typed: a bare name may introduce a source, a cross-reference,
a co-citation, or an editorial voice, and syntactic role is not
recoverable from surface patterns alone. The note on /tender-hefted/
(II.iv.168), for example, contains a naturally occurring two-level
citation — Furness quoting Knight disputing Steevens — embedded in
continuous prose without formal delimiters. This is the finding most
directly relevant to the Schmidt Sciences framing: reliable citation
extraction from this material requires reading comprehension, not
pattern matching.

David's reply to our inquiry about the Institutional Books dataset is
encouraging. He recommends beginning with a targeted bibliography —
a high-recall collection of Furness-cited works — rather than indexing
IB1 in its entirety, and notes that periodical sources such as the
/Edinburgh Review/ may be better retrieved from the Internet Archive,
which indexes individual issues more reliably than IB1 does. For the
83 unresolved citations, he suggests agentic search as an immediate
tactic to obtain high-confidence resolutions before formal indexing
infrastructure is in place. We intend to begin that work now.

For larger-scale work, David recommends Anserini (built on Lucene),
which supports both keyword and dense-vector retrieval, exposed as an
MCP server or local search skill. Building and querying embedding
indexes over a corpus the size of IB1 benefits substantially from GPU
resources, and the same infrastructure would support the AI-assisted
edge classification that is the project's longer-term research goal —
argument-relation tagging between annotations (rebuts, extends,
silently absorbs, and so forth). We would welcome Peter's guidance on
what is feasible within Tufts' HPC environment, and would be glad to
discuss the specific resource requirements when the time comes.
